Standard 3.2.2A record keeping, explained for small food businesses

Australia guide · As of October 2026

The short answer. Standard 3.2.2A came in from December 2023. If you are a category one business — a caterer or food service business that prepares unpackaged, potentially hazardous food into ready-to-eat food — you need a certified food safety supervisor, trained food handlers, and daily records that prove you managed the key food safety risks, kept for at least three months. Category two businesses need the supervisor and trained food handlers.

What Standard 3.2.2A is

Food Safety Standard 3.2.2A is part of the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code. It adds three "food safety management tools" for food service, catering and related retail businesses in Australia. According to Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ), the requirements came in in December 2023, and the standard generally covers caterers, restaurants, cafés, takeaway shops, pubs, supermarkets and delis, food vans and other places serving food (FSANZ: overview of the food safety management tools).

Despite the name of the Code, this standard is for businesses in Australia. New Zealand businesses work under their own food safety system, which this guide does not cover.

Category one or category two?

FSANZ defines the two categories by what you do with the food, not by the type of premises:

CategoryFSANZ definitionTools required
One (higher risk)A caterer or food service business that processes unpackaged potentially hazardous food into food that is ready to eat.Food safety supervisor, food handler training, and records (the "evidence tool").
TwoA retailer of potentially hazardous, ready-to-eat food handled unpackaged, where the food is not made or processed on site other than slicing, weighing, repacking, reheating or hot-holding.Food safety supervisor and food handler training.

As a rough illustration, a café that makes its own sandwiches and salads fits the category one description, while a shop that only slices and sells bought-in ham looks more like category two. If you are not sure which you are, ask your local council — they are the ones who will check.

The food safety supervisor

Category one and two businesses must appoint a certified food safety supervisor (FSS) before carrying out a "prescribed activity" — handling unpackaged, high-risk, ready-to-eat food. The certificate must have been obtained within the past five years, through a registered training organisation or an organisation recognised by your food regulator. The FSS must be "reasonably available": FSANZ describes this as working on site and overseeing food handling, or being easy for staff and authorised officers to contact, such as by phone (FSANZ: food safety supervisor).

In a one-person business, that person is usually you.

Record keeping: the evidence tool

This is the part that changes the day-to-day for category one businesses. FSANZ's guidance on the evidence tool (as of October 2026) says:

  • What: a record that proves you properly managed food safety risks when carrying out prescribed activities — safely receiving, storing, processing, displaying and transporting potentially hazardous food, and cleaning and sanitising.
  • When: each day the business carries out those activities.
  • What each record includes: the date, and the time where that matters.
  • Format: any form, including electronic — paper or electronic templates, or information recorded another way, as long as it is kept for the required time.
  • How long: at least three months.

FSANZ's guide Safe Food Australia contains detailed guidance on the standards, and the evidence tool page notes that templates a business may use are in its Appendix 8.

What temperatures to record against

FSANZ's temperature control fact sheet says potentially hazardous food must be at 5°C or colder, or 60°C or hotter, when it is received, displayed, transported or stored — unless you can show an enforcement officer a safe alternative system. Cooked food being cooled must go from 60°C to 21°C within two hours, and from 21°C to 5°C within a further four hours (FSANZ: temperature control requirements).

These differ from the UK figures you will see in many apps and templates written for Britain (8°C for chilled food and 63°C for hot holding). If you use a tool designed elsewhere, set the targets to the Australian ones.

A simple daily routine that covers it

Mapping FSANZ's prescribed activities onto a working day, a category one kitchen might record:

  • Receiving: temperature of chilled or hot potentially hazardous food on arrival, and what you did if it was outside 5°C / 60°C.
  • Storing: fridge and cool room readings, at least daily.
  • Processing: times and temperatures for batches you cook, cool down or reheat.
  • Displaying: readings for display fridges and hot holding during service.
  • Cleaning and sanitising: what was cleaned and sanitised, and when.
  • Corrective action: next to any reading that was wrong, what you did.

This list is a starting point, not an official checklist. Ask your council whether they expect anything more for your type of business — they are the people who will look at your records.

Where Probe fits

Probe is a daily food safety record for kitchens where one person does the checks: opening and closing checklists you can edit, temperature readings for each unit, diary notes for deliveries, cleaning, faults and illness, and a daily sign-off.
