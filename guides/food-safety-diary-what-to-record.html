What to write in your food safety diary every day

UK guide · Updated October 2026

The short answer. Every day you trade, record that your opening and closing checks were done, the temperatures of your fridges, freezers and any hot holding, and a note of anything that went wrong together with what you did about it. Sign it off each day, and look back over the last four weeks regularly. That is the pattern the Food Standards Agency's own diary is built around.

Why you have to keep a diary at all

Every food business in the UK needs a food safety management system based on HACCP principles. GOV.UK puts the record-keeping part plainly: you must keep records of your food safety systems, your checks, and how you have put things right — and if your business is ever involved in a food safety incident, you will need to show those records as evidence of how you keep food safe (GOV.UK: food safety management systems).

For small caterers you do not have to design that system from a blank page. In England and Wales, the FSA's Safer Food, Better Business packs set out the procedures and include a diary for the daily records. In Northern Ireland the equivalent is Safe Catering, and in Scotland Food Standards Scotland publishes CookSafe. Whichever you follow, the diary is where the day-to-day evidence lives.

What the FSA diary asks for each day

The introduction to the SFBB diary (PDF, as of October 2026) says the manager should sign the diary every day to confirm two things: the opening and closing checks have been done, and your safe methods have been followed. It also says the diary should take about a minute a day unless there is something special to write down.

That one minute is the routine. The entries that actually matter are the exceptions: the FSA asks you to note anything different that happens, or anything that goes wrong, along with what you did — so that you can show you acted to keep food safe.

Opening checks

The FSA's suggested opening list covers, in short: fridges, chilled displays and freezers working properly; other equipment such as ovens working; staff fit for work and in clean clothes; preparation areas clean and disinfected; no sign of pests; enough hand-washing and cleaning supplies; hot water at every sink and basin; a working probe thermometer with wipes; and accurate allergen information for everything on sale. You can add your own.

Closing checks

At the end of the day the list turns to putting things away safely: food covered, labelled and refrigerated where needed; food on its use-by date thrown away; dirty cleaning equipment cleaned or binned; waste removed; surfaces cleaned and disinfected; washing up finished; floors clean; and any "prove it" temperature checks recorded.

Temperatures

The SFBB chilled storage method (PDF) says to check your fridges and chilled display equipment at least once a day, starting with your opening checks. It recommends setting them at 5°C or below so that chilled food stays at 8°C or below — a legal requirement in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and recommended in Scotland. The FSA's "Prove it" sheet (PDF) adds that hot food must be kept at or above 63°C and that frozen food should be kept at -18°C or below. Our fridge temperature guide covers the detail.

What a good "something went wrong" entry looks like

This is the part of a paper diary most often left blank, and it is the part that shows you are in control. A useful entry answers three questions: what happened, what you did, and what happens next. For example:

  • Weak: "Fridge 2 warm."
  • Useful: "07:40 display fridge read 10°C. Door had been left ajar overnight. Probed the stock: dairy at 9°C, moved to the under-counter fridge. Rechecked at 08:40: 4°C. Reminder added to closing checks to check the door."

The FSA's chilled storage sheet spells out what it expects when a fridge fails: use other equipment or move the food somewhere cold; if you cannot, or do not know how long the equipment has been broken, contact the environmental health team at your council for advice; throw away chilled food that has been out of refrigeration for more than four hours; and write down what went wrong and what you did about it in your diary.

The 4-weekly review

The SFBB diary includes a 4-weekly review: a chance to look back over recent weeks, spot problems that keep coming back, and write down how you will tackle them — for example retraining someone or changing how a task is done. If the same fridge appears in your "went wrong" notes three times in a month, the review is where you decide whether it needs an engineer rather than another note.

Paper or digital?

The FSA's SFBB page offers both: print the diary pages and fill them in, or download them to your device, fill them in and store them electronically. Either way, completed pages should be kept safely until your next visit from a local authority food safety officer (GOV.UK: SFBB). If you want to keep your daily records in a different digital tool rather than the FSA's own pages, it is sensible to ask your council's food safety team how they would like to see them at an inspection.

Common gaps worth closing

  • Days with no entry at all, especially busy weekends.
  • Temperatures written in a neat column at the end of the week — the same pen, the same handwriting, every reading 3°C.
  • A high reading with nothing written next to it.
  • Sign-offs missing on the days the owner was away.
  • No review for months, even though the same problem keeps appearing.

None of these need anything more than a minute a day and the habit of writing down the action, not just the problem.
