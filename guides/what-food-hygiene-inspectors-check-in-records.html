What a food hygiene inspector looks for in your records

UK guide · Updated October 2026

The short answer. A food hygiene inspector looks at how you handle food, the condition of your premises, and how you manage food safety. Your records are the main evidence for that third part: they show the checks were done day after day, and — most importantly — what you did when something went wrong.

How the food hygiene rating is worked out

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme gives businesses a rating from 0 to 5. GOV.UK says the officer checks three elements: how hygienically food is handled, the physical condition of the premises, and how the business manages food safety — looking at processes, training and systems, so they can judge how confident they are that standards will be kept up in future (GOV.UK: Food Hygiene Rating Scheme).

A 5 means hygiene standards are very good and fully comply with the law; 0 means urgent improvement is necessary. Businesses in Wales are legally required to display their rating in a prominent place, and in Northern Ireland at or near each customer entrance. In England display is voluntary but encouraged.

Why records carry so much weight

An inspector sees your kitchen for an hour or two. Your records are the only evidence of the other days. GOV.UK says food businesses must keep records of their food safety systems, their checks, and how they have put things right — and that if you are involved in a food safety incident you will need to show those records as evidence of how you keep food safe (GOV.UK: food safety management systems).

Officers can also take copies of records, including computer records, and documents relating to the business — one London council lists this among the things an officer may do during a visit (Southwark Council: what happens during an inspection). In practice, whatever you keep should be easy to show and easy to copy, whether it is on paper or on a screen.

What they are likely to ask to see

If you follow the FSA's Safer Food, Better Business pack for caterers, it is made up of pieces an inspector will expect to find (GOV.UK: SFBB for caterers):

  • Your safe methods — the written procedures for how you handle cross-contamination, cleaning, chilling and cooking, plus management topics such as opening and closing checks.
  • Daily diary pages — opening and closing checks signed off each day.
  • "Prove it" records — temperature checks that show your methods work.
  • The 4-weekly review — evidence you look back and fix recurring problems.
  • Staff training record, suppliers' list and cleaning schedule.

Northern Ireland's Safe Catering and Scotland's CookSafe have their own equivalents.

What makes records believable

The quality that matters is not neat handwriting — it is records that look like they were made on the day, by someone who was paying attention:

  • Problems are written down, with actions. The SFBB diary introduction asks you to note anything that went wrong and what you did, so you can show you acted to keep food safe (PDF). A diary with no problems in six months is harder to believe than one with a few, each dealt with.
  • Readings vary. Real fridges read 3°C one day and 6°C the next. A column of identical figures invites questions.
  • Gaps are explained. A closed day should look like a closed day, not a missing one.
  • The review is real. If the same fridge keeps appearing, the review should say what you decided to do about it.
  • The probe is checked. The FSA's "Prove it" sheet describes testing a digital probe in iced and boiling water, and suggests once a month if the manufacturer gives no schedule (PDF).

A checklist before the next visit

  • Can you find the last few months of diary pages or records in under a minute?
  • Is every trading day signed off? If not, do you know which ones are missing?
  • Does every out-of-range temperature have an action written next to it?
  • Is there a recent 4-weekly review?
  • Are your safe methods still accurate for your current menu, suppliers and equipment? GOV.UK says to check, review and update your system regularly, and whenever your food processes or suppliers change.
  • Are the training record and suppliers' list up to date?
  • If your records are digital, can you show or hand over a copy without fuss on the spot?

After the inspection

GOV.UK notes that a new rating is given at each inspection, and that anyone can see the full inspection report for a business by making a Freedom of Information request to the council that inspected it. If you disagree with your rating, your council's food safety team can explain how to appeal or ask for a re-visit.
